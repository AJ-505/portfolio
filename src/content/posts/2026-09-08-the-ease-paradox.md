I have experienced a few things that have made me wonder. For example:

- Free courses and programs tend to be less effective than paid ones, even when the content is the same.
- Christian converts tend to take the faith more seriously than cradle Christians.
- Things like AI overviews and summaries, if not used correctly, can actually _hamper_ our depth of understanding of the information we consume.
- More broadly, some of the most up-and-doing people I know have had to earn their place by going through tough experiences that have shaped them.

I call it **The Ease Paradox.** It is especially applicable when it pertains to learning or any kind of human development. The hypothesis, simply put:

> By making things easier for people, most of the time, you (paradoxically) make it harder for them.

## Why does this even happen?

### 1. Many times, the effort _is_ where learning happens

Making any activity easier to perform generally reduces the effort needed to perform the activity. If that effort is _instrumental_ to making anyone improve at the action (e.g learning a skill), it may hamper one's ability to learn or grow.

### 2. Shortcuts lead to long delays
For every shortcut you take today, there's a good chance it costs you tomorrow. It's not the best use of your time to do something in the present that might hurt in future.

## The Nuance

Not all effort is useful. It is important to separate _productive struggle_ from _tedium._ Although in my experience, especially when I'm starting out learning something, it is hard to separate between the two. In other words, you have to learn about something long enough to know what moves the needle and what doesn't.

Moreso, I believe this goes against the world view of _"Work smart, not hard"_. I have always found this ridiculous because it proposes that you should seek a faster method of doing something from the start, before you even know _a way_, not to talk of _the right way_ of doing the thing.

## What can we do about it?

### Increase the friction, if it helps 

Instead of trying to make something _easier_ for yourself, perhaps try making it _more challenging_ and _more enjoyable_. I think the dividends of both sides (the challenge and the joy) pay off massively in the long run. Instead of seeking _ease_ (especially if the simplification came from an external source), put in the effort to break down complicated things, and then deal with the easier problems that ensue.


### Be careful with your privileges

It was [fascinating to hear from a world-class mathematician](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZAzWPE9CItg&t=11756s) on how not learning basic math brought great suffering on the world. Doing some reading, I found that the kids who didn't learn how to do those basic calculations struggled to make basic estimates, lacked basic understanding of numbers and their relations, and were even more vulnerable. That scared me to the bone. So much for the ease the calculator brought!

### See hardship as an advantage

Admittedly not easy to do in practice. But having a [Growth Mindset](https://ctl.stanford.edu/students/growth-mindset) helps a lot here. Having a big hurdle in front of you is a blessing - you get the opportunity to become exponentially better, even if you fail. Start thinking of challenges that way.

### Embrace the Grind

Perhaps the most important thing here. Work hard. Lock in. Push yourself past your limits. And surround yourself with people who do too.

### Understand the nuance, and make sure your effort counts

You might be getting stuck on a problem you simply can't solve by putting in more hours. You may be wasting time going round in circles on something a simple question may resolve. You may be attributing time or effort to a problem that requires _technique_ or _strategy_. Learn to notice such problems.
